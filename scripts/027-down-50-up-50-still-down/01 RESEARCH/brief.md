# Brief: why down 50% then up 50% leaves you down 25%

Series: Simple Proofs
Idea: A percent change is a percent OF the amount at that moment, so after halving $100 to $50, a 50% gain adds only $25: you end at $75. Percent changes multiply (× 0.5 × 1.5 = × 0.75); they don't add.
Misconception: Down 50% and then up 50% cancel out and bring you back to where you started.
Hook angle: Lose half your money, gain fifty percent back, and you're still down a quarter.
Narrowed to: One amount, two equal-size percent moves. No compound interest, no averages of returns.
Key condition to say aloud: Each percent is of the current amount (the usual meaning for prices, savings and investments).
Candidate sources: OpenStax, Prealgebra 2e, §6.1 Understand Percent (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-prealgebra-bundle/blob/main/modules/m81306/index.cnxml); OpenStax, Prealgebra 2e, §6.2 Solve General Applications of Percent (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-prealgebra-bundle/blob/main/modules/m81313/index.cnxml); OpenStax, Contemporary Mathematics, §6.2 Discounts, Markups, and Sales Tax (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-contemporary-mathematics/blob/main/modules/m00058/index.cnxml)
Originality: Usually given as a trick question. Ours shows the two percentages acting on two different-sized bars, so the viewer sees the second 50% is half of a smaller bar.
Not used: Investment-return averages (arithmetic vs geometric mean: true, but a different Short); real stock examples (would need sources).
