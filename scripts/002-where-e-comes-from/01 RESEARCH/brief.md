# Brief: where the number e comes from

Series: One Equation
Idea: e is what one dollar at 100% yearly interest becomes when the interest is added more and more often: (1 + 1/n)^n creeps up but levels off at about 2.718, because each extra split adds interest on interest but in ever-thinner slices.
Misconception: Compounding more and more often should make the money grow without limit.
Hook angle: A dollar at 100% interest, compounded every second, still ends the year under $2.72.
Narrowed to: One dollar, one year, 100% interest, compounded n times. No calculus, no e^x, no proof that the limit exists (the table shows it settling).
Key condition to say aloud: 100% interest for exactly one year, interest added in n equal slices.
Candidate sources: OpenStax, Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §6.1 Exponential Functions (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-algebra-bundle/blob/main/modules/m49361/index.cnxml); OpenStax, Calculus Volume 1, §1.5 Exponential and Logarithmic Functions (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-calculus-bundle/blob/main/modules/m53481/index.cnxml); Ohio State University, Mooculus Calculus, Numbers and Taylor series (https://github.com/mooculus/calculus/blob/master/numbersAndTaylorSeries/digInNumbersAndTaylorSeries.tex); APEX Calculus, §8.1 Sequences (Hartman et al., Virginia Military Institute) (https://github.com/APEXCalculus/APEXCalculus_Source/blob/master/text/08_Sequences.tex)
Originality: Usually e is introduced as 'a famous constant' or via derivatives. Ours builds the formula symbol by symbol from the bank example and makes the levelling-off the surprise; script B races the compounding frequencies.
Not used: Euler naming e (true and two-sourced, but costs words); e's series 1 + 1 + 1/2! + ...; e is irrational (sourced in one book only here: Algebra and Trigonometry; not needed).
