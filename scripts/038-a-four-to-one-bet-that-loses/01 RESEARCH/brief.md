# Brief: why a bet paying 4 to 1 can still lose you money

Series: Brain Breakers
Idea: Expected value weighs each result by its chance: a die bet that pays $4 on a six and costs $1 otherwise has expected value 4 × 1/6 − 1 × 5/6 = −1/6, a loss of about 17 cents per roll on average; 5 to 1 would be exactly fair.
Misconception: A big payout (4 to 1) means a good bet.
Hook angle: A bet that pays four to one on a die roll still loses you money.
Narrowed to: One invented die bet. No real casino games (their exact rules would need sources), no variance.
Key condition to say aloud: A fair six-sided die; 'loses money' means on average over many rolls.
Candidate sources: OpenStax, Contemporary Mathematics, §7.11 Expected Value (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-contemporary-mathematics/blob/main/modules/m00097/index.cnxml); OpenIntro Statistics (Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel & Barr), ch. 3 Probability (https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/openintro-statistics/blob/master/ch_probability/TeX/ch_probability.tex); OpenStax, Introductory Statistics 2e, §4.2 Mean or Expected Value and Standard Deviation (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-introductory-statistics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m46959/index.cnxml)
Originality: Textbooks use roulette, keno or lotteries. Ours uses a single die bet a friend might offer, and B finds the fair payout (5 to 1) by making the one win cover the five losses.
Not used: 'Every casino game has a negative expected value for the player' (Contemporary Mathematics says this; one source, so pinned comment only as a hint, not a claim); real roulette odds.
