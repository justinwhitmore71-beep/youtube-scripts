# Brief: why the worst scorers improve even if you do nothing

Series: Brain Breakers
Idea: People picked because of an extreme score tend to land closer to average next time, even with no change at all, because part of the extreme score was chance and chance doesn't carry over: regression to the mean.
Misconception: If the worst performers improve after you do something, the thing you did worked (and if the best get worse, they got lazy).
Hook angle: Pick the worst scorers, do nothing at all, and on average they improve next time.
Narrowed to: Repeated test scores with a random part. No regression lines, no correlation coefficients.
Key condition to say aloud: 'On average': individuals can still go either way; the effect needs scores that are partly chance.
Candidate sources: Poldrack, Statistical Thinking for the 21st Century, ch. 14 The General Linear Model: Regression to the mean (Stanford University) (https://github.com/statsthinking21/statsthinking21-core/blob/master/14-GeneralLinearModel.Rmd); Adhikari, DeNero & Wagner, Computational and Inferential Thinking (UC Berkeley Data 8), §15.2 The Regression Line (https://github.com/data-8/textbook/blob/main/chapters/15/2/Regression_Line.ipynb)
Originality: Usually explained with sports 'curses' or Galton's heights. Ours uses a plain score = skill + luck picture and shows the luck bar re-rolling while the skill bar stays.
Not used: 'Always use a control group' (Statistical Thinking says this; only one source opened, so the takeaway is phrased as advice instead); Galton's heights (Data 8 has it; a separate Short).
