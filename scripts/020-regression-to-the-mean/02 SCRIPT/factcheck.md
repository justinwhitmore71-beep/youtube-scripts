# Fact-check: why the worst scorers improve even if you do nothing (both scripts)

| # | Claim | Source 1 | Source 2 | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| c1 | People selected for extreme (very low or very high) scores tend, on average, to score closer to the group average next time, even with no real change; this is called regression to the mean. | [Poldrack, Statistical Thinking for the 21st Century, ch. 14 The General Linear Model: Regression to the mean (Stanford University)](https://github.com/statsthinking21/statsthinking21-core/blob/master/14-GeneralLinearModel.Rmd): "If we select just those subjects on the basis of their first test scores, they are guaranteed to move back towards the mean of the entire group on the second test, even if there is no effect of training." | [Adhikari, DeNero & Wagner, Computational and Inferential Thinking (UC Berkeley Data 8), §15.2 The Regression Line](https://github.com/data-8/textbook/blob/main/chapters/15/2/Regression_Line.ipynb): "In general, individuals who are away from average on one variable are expected to be not quite as far away from average on the other. This is called the *regression effect*." | OK |
| c2 | Part of an extreme score is chance (luck on the day), and that part does not carry over to the next test, while the skill part does. | [Poldrack, Statistical Thinking for the 21st Century, ch. 14 The General Linear Model: Regression to the mean (Stanford University)](https://github.com/statsthinking21/statsthinking21-core/blob/master/14-GeneralLinearModel.Rmd): "What has happened is that some students scored badly on the first test simply due to random chance." | [Adhikari, DeNero & Wagner, Computational and Inferential Thinking (UC Berkeley Data 8), §15.2 The Regression Line](https://github.com/data-8/textbook/blob/main/chapters/15/2/Regression_Line.ipynb): "we predict that the child will be somewhat closer to average than the parents were. This is called "regression to the mean"" | OK |

Notes: c2: Statistical Thinking (source 1) gives the chance explanation directly, in a simulation where scores are pure chance; Data 8 (source 2) supports the 'closer to average next time' half, in its parents-and-children heights example, and does not mention luck. 'Score = skill + luck' is the script's simplified picture of 'a stable part plus a random part'. Statistical Thinking also says an untreated control group is needed to avoid being 'tricked by regression to the mean' (one source, so not used as a claim).
