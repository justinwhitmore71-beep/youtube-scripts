# Brief: why we always see the same side of the Moon

Series: The Universe, Explained
Idea: The Moon spins on its axis exactly once per orbit (about 27.3 days), so the same face stays turned toward Earth; a Moon that didn't spin at all would show us every side as it went round.
Misconception: We always see the same side because the Moon doesn't spin.
Hook angle: We always see the same side of the Moon, and that's because it spins, not because it doesn't.
Narrowed to: Why one face points at us. No tidal-locking mechanism (not two-sourced here), no libration.
Key condition to say aloud: Spin period equal to orbital period (about 27.3 days, measured against the stars).
Candidate sources: OpenStax, Astronomy 2e, §4.5 Phases and Motions of the Moon (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-astronomy/blob/main/modules/m59788/index.cnxml); OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, §11.4 Precession of a Gyroscope (problems) (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-university-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m58338/index.cnxml)
Originality: Usually stated as a fact with the term 'tidally locked'. Ours starts from the wrong guess and shows the non-spinning Moon turning its far side to us halfway round, then the spinning one that doesn't.
Not used: Why the spin and orbit got matched (tidal locking: not two-sourced in this session); libration (we actually see about 59%); the name 'synchronous rotation' (one source).
