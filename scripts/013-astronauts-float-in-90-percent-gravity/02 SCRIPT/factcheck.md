| c2 | Gravity weakens with the square of the distance from Earth's centre, but at 400 km up it is still about 8.7 m/s², almost 90% of the 9.8 m/s² at the surface. | [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, §13.2 Gravitation Near Earth's Surface (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-university-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m58346/index.cnxml): "at 400 km above Earth’s surface, where the International Space Station orbits, the value of g is 8.67m/s2." | [OpenStax, Astronomy 2e, §3.4 Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-astronomy/blob/main/modules/m59777/index.cnxml): "the magnitude of the force of gravity must decrease with increasing distance between the Sun and a planet (or between any two objects) in proportion to the inverse square of their separation." | OK |
| c3 | Astronauts float ('weightless') because they and their spacecraft are in free fall together. | [OpenStax, Astronomy 2e, §3.4 Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-astronomy/blob/main/modules/m59777/index.cnxml): "The astronauts feel “weightless” (meaning that they don’t feel the gravitational force acting on them) for the same reason that passengers in an elevator whose cable has broken feel weightless: they are falling" | [OpenStax, College Physics 2e, §4.3 Newton's Second Law of Motion: Concept of a System (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m42073/index.cnxml): "When they speak of “weightlessness” and “microgravity,” they are really referring to the phenomenon we call “free-fall” in physics." | OK |
| c4 | An orbiting object falls continuously, but Earth curves away beneath it at the same rate, so it never hits the ground. | [OpenStax, College Physics 2e, §3.4 Projectile Motion (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m42042/index.cnxml): "When an object is in orbit, the Earth curves away from underneath the object at the same rate as it falls. | c5 | The station moves sideways at nearly 8 km/s (about 7.7 km/s). | [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, §13.4 Satellite Orbits and Energy (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-university-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m58348/index.cnxml): "= 7.67 × 103m/s which is about 17,000 mph." | [OpenStax, Astronomy 2e, §3.6 Motions of Satellites and Spacecraft (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-astronomy/blob/main/modules/m59779/index.cnxml): "The speed needed to do this—called the circular satellite velocity—is about 8 kilometers per second" | OK |

Notes: c2 derived: 8.67 / 9.80 = 0.885, 'almost ninety percent'. Check with the inverse-square law: (6,371 / (6,371 + 400))² = (6,371 / 6,771)² = 0.885, the same. The station's altitude varies; sources give 400 km as the typical/average value. Astronomy 2e §3.4 also says gravity 'a few hundred kilometers above' the surface 'is certainly not a great deal weaker'. Quotes from the OpenStax source files on GitHub; '102' and '103' are 10² and 10³ written without superscripts.
