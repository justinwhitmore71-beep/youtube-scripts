# Brief: why moving together doesn't mean one causes the other

Series: Brain Breakers
Idea: Two quantities can rise and fall together (be correlated) because a third, hidden factor drives both (a confounding variable), so correlation alone can't show that one causes the other; a randomized experiment is what can.
Misconception: If two things move together, one must be causing the other.
Hook angle: Umbrella sales and traffic jams can rise together without either one causing the other.
Narrowed to: One invented example (rain drives umbrella sales and traffic jams). No correlation coefficient formula.
Key condition to say aloud: The example is hypothetical; the point is the logic, not real sales data.
Candidate sources: Adhikari, DeNero & Wagner, Computational and Inferential Thinking (UC Berkeley Data 8), §15.1 Correlation (https://github.com/data-8/textbook/blob/main/chapters/15/1/Correlation.ipynb); OpenIntro, Introduction to Modern Statistics (Çetinkaya-Rundel & Hardin), Study design: Confounding variable (https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/ims/blob/main/data-design.qmd); OpenIntro Statistics (Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel & Barr), ch. 1 Introduction to data: Experiments (https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/openintro-statistics/blob/master/ch_intro_to_data/TeX/ch_intro_to_data.tex); OpenIntro, Introduction to Modern Statistics (Çetinkaya-Rundel & Hardin), Hello data: Experiments (https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/ims/blob/main/data-hello.qmd)
Originality: Textbooks use ice cream and drownings or boating accidents. Ours uses umbrellas and traffic jams on rainy days, and draws the hidden driver as a third arrow on screen.
Not used: Real spurious-correlation datasets (would need sources); the correlation coefficient r (Data 8 covers it; separate Short).
