# Fact-check: why moving together doesn't mean one causes the other (both scripts)

| # | Claim | Source 1 | Source 2 | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| c1 | Correlation (two things moving together) only measures association; it does not by itself show that one causes the other. | [Adhikari, DeNero & Wagner, Computational and Inferential Thinking (UC Berkeley Data 8), §15.1 Correlation](https://github.com/data-8/textbook/blob/main/chapters/15/1/Correlation.ipynb): "Correlation only measures association. Correlation does not imply causation." | [OpenIntro Statistics (Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel & Barr), ch. 1 Introduction to data: Experiments](https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/openintro-statistics/blob/master/ch_intro_to_data/TeX/ch_intro_to_data.tex): "In general, association does not imply causation, and causation can only be inferred from a randomized experiment." | OK |
| c2 | A third variable associated with both (a confounding variable) can make two things move together and prevents concluding that one causes the other. | [OpenIntro, Introduction to Modern Statistics (Çetinkaya-Rundel & Hardin), Study design: Confounding variable](https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/ims/blob/main/data-design.qmd): "A **confounding variable** ... is one that is associated with both the explanatory and response variables. Because it is associated with both variables, it prevents the study from concluding that the explanatory variable caused the response variable." | [Adhikari, DeNero & Wagner, Computational and Inferential Thinking (UC Berkeley Data 8), §15.1 Correlation](https://github.com/data-8/textbook/blob/main/chapters/15/1/Correlation.ipynb): "Age is a confounding variable: older children are both heavier and better at math than younger children, on average." | OK |
| c3 | Hypothetical example: in a city, umbrella sales and traffic jams rise and fall together because rain drives both. | Invented illustration, not a data claim: shown on screen as two made-up lines that both follow a third 'rain' line. | - | PROOF |
| c4 | In a randomized experiment, the possible cause is assigned at random, which makes the groups roughly equal in other respects and so controls for confounding variables. | [OpenIntro, Introduction to Modern Statistics (Çetinkaya-Rundel & Hardin), Hello data: Experiments](https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/ims/blob/main/data-hello.qmd): "Random assignment organizes the participants in a study into groups that are roughly equal on all aspects, thus allowing us to control for any confounding variables that might affect the outcome" | [OpenIntro Statistics (Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel & Barr), ch. 1 Introduction to data: Experiments](https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/openintro-statistics/blob/master/ch_intro_to_data/TeX/ch_intro_to_data.tex): "When individuals are randomly assigned to a group, the experiment is called a randomized experiment." | OK |
