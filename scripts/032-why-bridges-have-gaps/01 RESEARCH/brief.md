# Brief: why bridges have expansion joints

Series: Everyday Physics
Idea: Solids grow slightly when they warm: ΔL = αLΔT. Steel's α is only 12 millionths per degree, but over a 1,275 m span and a 55 °C range that's 0.84 m, so bridges need expansion joints to grow without buckling.
Misconception: Solid steel doesn't change size; the gaps in bridges are flaws or wear.
Hook angle: A steel span over a kilometer long grows about eighty-four centimeters from freezing to hot.
Narrowed to: Linear expansion of one steel span (the textbooks' 1,275 m, −15 °C to 40 °C example). No volume expansion, no atomic explanation.
Key condition to say aloud: The textbook example treats the span as all steel over a 55 °C temperature range.
Candidate sources: OpenStax, College Physics 2e, §13.2 Thermal Expansion of Solids and Liquids (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m42215/index.cnxml); OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, §1.3 Thermal Expansion (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-university-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m58386/index.cnxml)
Originality: Usually stated as 'metals expand when hot'. Ours builds ΔL = αLΔT from the tiny α to the big answer, so the viewer sees why a small number becomes 84 cm.
Not used: The atomic reason (atoms move farther apart: University Physics Vol. 2 explains it, College Physics does not, so one source); railway buckling stories; the Eiffel Tower height change (not sourced here).
