# Brief: why 1 + 2 + ... + 100 = 5050

Series: Simple Proofs
Idea: Pair the ends of 1 + 2 + ... + 100: 1 + 100, 2 + 99, 3 + 98 all make 101, because moving inward one side gains 1 as the other loses 1; fifty pairs of 101 is 5050.
Misconception: You'd have to do a hundred separate additions.
Hook angle: Add every number from one to a hundred, and the answer takes five seconds.
Narrowed to: Consecutive whole numbers from 1. B states the general n(n + 1)/2 on screen. No story about who first did it.
Key condition to say aloud: Consecutive numbers (an evenly spaced list); 100 is even, so the numbers pair up exactly.
Candidate sources: Levin, Discrete Mathematics: An Open Introduction, Sequences chapter, section 'Rate of Growth' (University of Northern Colorado) (https://github.com/oscarlevin/discrete-book/blob/main/source/sec_seq-growth.ptx); Ohio State University, Mooculus Calculus, Sigma notation (https://github.com/mooculus/calculus/blob/master/approximatingTheAreaUnderACurve/digInSigmaNotation.tex); OpenStax, Algebra and Trigonometry 2e, §13.4 Series and Their Notations (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-algebra-bundle/blob/main/modules/m49447/index.cnxml)
Originality: The pairing trick is famous as a childhood anecdote. Ours skips the story (only one source for it) and shows WHY every pair is the same: one side climbs as the other falls. B shows the forward-and-backward rows as a rectangle.
Not used: The Gauss-as-a-schoolboy story (one source opened: Levin, who says it is 'usually attributed'); triangular-number pictures; odd n.
