# Brief: why four rolls give only a 52% chance of a six

Series: Brain Breakers
Idea: Adding 1/6 four times double-counts rolls with more than one six, so it overshoots; instead find the chance of NO six, (5/6)⁴ ≈ 48%, and subtract from 1: about 52%. Even six rolls leave a one-in-three chance of no six.
Misconception: Four rolls give 4 × 1/6 = 2/3 chance of a six (and six rolls guarantee one).
Hook angle: Roll a die four times: the chance of at least one six is only about fifty-two percent.
Narrowed to: A fair die, 'at least one six' in 4 (A) or 6 (B) rolls. No history (de Méré), no binomial formula.
Key condition to say aloud: Fair die, independent rolls.
Candidate sources: OpenStax, Introductory Statistics 2e, §3.1 Terminology (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-introductory-statistics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m46942/index.cnxml); OpenIntro Statistics (Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel & Barr), ch. 3 Probability (https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/openintro-statistics/blob/master/ch_probability/TeX/ch_probability.tex); OpenStax, Introductory Statistics 2e, §3.3 Two Basic Rules of Probability (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-introductory-statistics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m46947/index.cnxml); OpenStax, Introductory Statistics 2e, §3.2 Independent and Mutually Exclusive Events (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-introductory-statistics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m46948/index.cnxml)
Originality: Usually solved with a formula. Ours names why adding fails (double sixes counted twice) and then flips the question to 'no six at all'.
Not used: The 17th-century gambling story behind this problem (not sourced here); the binomial distribution.
