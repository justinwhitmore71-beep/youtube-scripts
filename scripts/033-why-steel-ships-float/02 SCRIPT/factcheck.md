# Fact-check: why a steel ship floats when a steel bolt sinks (both scripts)

| # | Claim | Source 1 | Source 2 | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| c1 | Archimedes' principle: the upward (buoyant) force on an object in a fluid equals the weight of the fluid it pushes aside (displaces); if that is more than the object's weight, it rises or floats. | [OpenStax, College Physics 2e, §11.7 Archimedes’ Principle (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m42196/index.cnxml): "The buoyant force on an object equals the weight of the fluid it displaces." | [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, §14.4 Archimedes’ Principle and Buoyancy (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-university-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m58356/index.cnxml): "The buoyant force on an object equals the weight of the fluid it displaces." | OK |
| c2 | Shaping the same material into a hollow boat makes it push aside more water, so it gets a bigger upward push with the same weight; this is why steel ships float. | [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, §14.4 Archimedes’ Principle and Buoyancy (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-university-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m58356/index.cnxml): "Because of its shape, the clay boat displaces more water than the lump and experiences a greater buoyant force, even though its mass is the same. The same is true of steel ships." | [OpenStax, College Physics 2e, §11.7 Archimedes’ Principle (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m42196/index.cnxml): "If the foil is folded to give it four sides, and paper clips or washers are added to this “boat,” what shape of the boat would allow it to hold the most “cargo” when placed in water?" | OK |
| c3 | An object floats if its average density (total mass ÷ total volume, including the air inside a hull) is less than the water's; it settles when the water pushed aside weighs as much as it does. | [OpenStax, College Physics 2e, §11.7 Archimedes’ Principle (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m42196/index.cnxml): "The average density of an object is what ultimately determines whether it floats. If its average density is less than that of the surrounding fluid, it will float." | [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, §14.4 Archimedes’ Principle and Buoyancy (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-university-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m58356/index.cnxml): "If the buoyant force equals the object’s weight, the object can remain suspended at its present depth, either immersed in the fluid (as for a neutrally buoyant diver) or partly above the surface (as with a floating object.)" | OK |
| c4 | A solid lump of steel sinks because steel is denser than water (7.8 g/mL vs 1.000). | [OpenStax, College Physics 2e, §11.2 Density (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m42187/index.cnxml): "Iron or steel 7.8" | [OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, §14.4 Archimedes’ Principle and Buoyancy (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-university-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m58356/index.cnxml): "Likewise, an object denser than the fluid will sink." | OK |

Notes: c2 source 2 is College Physics' own take-home foil-boat exercise (it supports the folding idea in B, not the claim about ships, which source 1 makes directly); the clay-boat example is University Physics'. c4: College Physics §11.2's density table lists 'Iron or steel 7.8' and 'Water (4ºC) 1.000' (in 10³ kg/m³); University Physics Vol. 1 §14.4 gives the 'denser sinks' rule. Both are OpenStax books (different author teams).
