# Brief: why a steel ship floats when a steel bolt sinks

Series: Everyday Physics
Idea: Water pushes up with a force equal to the weight of water an object pushes aside (Archimedes' principle). A hollow hull pushes aside far more water than a solid lump of the same steel, so the push grows until it matches the ship's weight: shape, not material, decides.
Misconception: Heavy, dense materials like steel always sink.
Hook angle: A steel bolt sinks, but a huge ship of the same steel floats.
Narrowed to: Floating vs sinking in still water, Archimedes' principle only. No stability, no submarines, no pressure derivation.
Key condition to say aloud: Still water; 'floats' means the hull pushes aside its own weight of water before the deck goes under.
Candidate sources: OpenStax, College Physics 2e, §11.7 Archimedes’ Principle (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m42196/index.cnxml); OpenStax, University Physics Volume 1, §14.4 Archimedes’ Principle and Buoyancy (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-university-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m58356/index.cnxml); OpenStax, College Physics 2e, §11.2 Density (Rice University) (https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-college-physics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m42187/index.cnxml)
Originality: Usually explained with a formula or a clay boat. Ours compares a bolt and a hull made of the same steel, and B uses a sheet of foil balled up vs folded into a tray.
Not used: 'Steel is 7.8 times denser than water' (the number is in one clean table only, College Physics §11.2); ship tonnages (would need sources).
