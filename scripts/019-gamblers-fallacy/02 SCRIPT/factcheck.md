# Fact-check: why tails is never 'due' (both scripts)

| # | Claim | Source 1 | Source 2 | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| c1 | Expecting a 'due' outcome after a streak is called the gambler's fallacy. | [OpenIntro Statistics (Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel & Barr), ch. 3 Probability](https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/openintro-statistics/blob/master/ch_probability/TeX/ch_probability.tex): "He has forgotten that the next roulette spin is independent of the previous spins. ... This is called the gambler's fallacy." | [Poldrack, Statistical Thinking for the 21st Century, ch. 8 Resampling and simulation: What is randomness? (Stanford University)](https://github.com/statsthinking21/statsthinking21-core/blob/master/08-Resampling.Rmd): "humans tend to think of random processes as self-correcting, which leads us to expect that we are "due for a win" after losing many rounds in a game of chance, a phenomenon known as the "gambler's fallacy"." | OK |
| c2 | Coin flips are independent: earlier flips don't change the chance (1/2 for a fair coin) of the next one. | [OpenStax, Contemporary Mathematics, §7.4 Tree Diagrams, Tables, and Outcomes (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-contemporary-mathematics/blob/main/modules/m00088/index.cnxml): "No matter what happens on the first flip, the second flip has the same sample space: {H, T} (You’ll sometimes hear the phrase “The coin has no memory”). So, these stages are independent." | [Poldrack, Statistical Thinking for the 21st Century, ch. 8 Resampling and simulation: What is randomness? (Stanford University)](https://github.com/statsthinking21/statsthinking21-core/blob/master/08-Resampling.Rmd): "if I flip a fair coin 10 times, the value of the outcome on one flip does not provide me with any information that lets me predict the outcome on the next flip." | OK |
| c3 | For independent flips, probabilities multiply, so any exact sequence of six fair flips (HHHHHH or HHHHHT) has probability (1/2)^6 = 1/64. | [OpenIntro Statistics (Diez, Çetinkaya-Rundel & Barr), ch. 3 Probability](https://github.com/OpenIntroStat/openintro-statistics/blob/master/ch_probability/TeX/ch_probability.tex): "If $A$ and $B$ represent events from two different and independent processes, then the probability that both $A$ and $B$ occur can be calculated as the product of their separate probabilities" | [OpenStax, Introductory Statistics 2e, §3.2 Independent and Mutually Exclusive Events (Rice University)](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-introductory-statistics-bundle/blob/main/modules/m46948/index.cnxml): "Two events A and B are independent events if the knowledge that one occurred does not affect the chance the other occurs." | OK |

Notes: c3 derived: (1/2)^6 = 1/64 for each specific sequence of six flips (multiplication rule for independent events). After five heads have happened, P(sixth is heads) = P(sixth is tails) = 1/2. The OpenIntro example is roulette (where black is slightly under 1/2 because of the green slots); the script uses a fair coin. Quotes from the books' source files on GitHub (OpenIntro's '$A$' is LaTeX for A). Pinned comment (law of large numbers): OpenIntro Statistics ch. 3: 'As more observations are collected, the proportion ... of occurrences with a particular outcome converges to the probability p of that outcome.'; Introductory Statistics 2e §2.5: 'if you take samples of larger and larger size from any population, then the mean x ¯ of the sample is very likely to get closer and closer to µ.'
